Phase 3 — 2026 Specific Investigation

An isolated forensic investigation of the GATE DA 2026 examination, organized by IIT Guwahati, exposes critical shifts in examiner psychology, pedagogical priorities, and psychometric targeting.2 The 2026 paper finalized the identity of the DA examination: it is not a test for software engineers who use data APIs, but a rigorous evaluation of applied mathematicians and statistical theorists.

The most striking revelation of the 2026 paper was the aggressive recalibration of Data Structures and Algorithms (DSA) and Programming. Unlike the legacy Computer Science (CS) paper, where algorithmic design is paramount, the DA 2026 paper reduced programming entirely to logical tracing, scope management, and foundational complexity proofs.1 Concurrently, Machine Learning surged to dominate the paper, representing an estimated 25-30% of the total weightage, intimately fused with Probability and Linear Algebra.2

Systematic Review of 2026 Cognitive Targeting

Q11 (PCA Orthogonality)

This question asked for the angle between the first and tenth principal components.1 The examiner included this to test the geometric foundations of Principal Component Analysis. It targeted the student weakness of viewing PCA merely as an algorithmic black box for dimensionality reduction, demanding the cognitive recognition that principal components, as eigenvectors of a symmetric covariance matrix, are strictly orthogonal ($90^\circ$).

Q13 (Uninformed Search Taxonomy)

By asking candidates to identify which algorithm is NOT uninformed, the setter tested foundational AI taxonomies.1 It targeted candidates who memorize search implementations without understanding the distinction between heuristic-guided (A*) and blind (BFS/DFS) state-space exploration.

Q14 & Q38 (Predicate Logic and Entailment)

IIT Guwahati placed a heavy emphasis on formal reasoning.1 Q38 required translating the natural language premise "A Creative person will Succeed if the person is also Disciplined, but will not succeed otherwise" into logical equivalence.12 This targeted the pervasive weakness among engineering students of relying on intuitive reading comprehension rather than strict propositional logic derivations.

Q15 (Quicksort Expected Time)

This question presented a modified Quicksort where the pivot is always the first element of a randomly ordered array, asking for the expected time recurrence relation.1 The examiner's intent was to punish rote memorization of the $O(n \log n)$ average case. The cognitive skill tested was the ability to derive an expectation based on probability distributions across subarrays, targeting the inability to formulate algorithms mathematically.

Q16 (Python Mutable Default Argument)

The code snippet def append_to_lst(val, lst=[]) tested practical programming semantics.1 The examiner targeted a specific, pervasive weakness in novice Python programmers: the assumption that default arguments are re-evaluated upon every function call. By testing this, IIT Guwahati selected for candidates who understand memory allocation and state persistence in interpreter-level execution.

Q21 (Rotation Matrix Powers)

Candidates were asked to compute $M^{2026}$ for a $2 \times 2$ rotation matrix with an angle of $2\pi/5$.1 This was the most surprising mathematical question, testing deep spatial abstraction. It targeted candidates who would attempt to multiply matrices brute-force. The cognitive requirement was recognizing that five rotations complete a full circle ($M^5 = I_2$), reducing the problem to modulo arithmetic.

Q42 (Relational Algebra Optimization)

Presenting a complex natural join expression, the examiner tested database query execution.1 The trap targeted the operational sequence. By forcing candidates to evaluate the Cartesian products, the examiner penalized those who failed to push the highly restrictive selection condition ($\sigma_{S>1}$) down the evaluation tree first, turning a time-sink into a rapid elimination.

Q52 (Projection Matrix Identification)

The matrix $M = I_n - \frac{1}{n}11^T$ was presented to test linear algebra maturity.1 The examiner targeted "notation panic"—the tendency of students to freeze when encountering dense mathematical formalism. The cognitive skill tested was recognizing standard centering matrices as idempotent projection matrices, immediately resolving the properties without algebraic expansion.

Q55 (Ridge Regression with MAE)

Perhaps the most lethal psychometric trap in the paper, this NAT question asked for the regularized loss function value using Mean Absolute Error (MAE) instead of the standard Mean Squared Error (MSE) associated with Ridge Regression.1 The examiner explicitly targeted muscle memory. Students reflexively squared the error term because "Ridge implies squared." The cognitive skill tested was rigid adherence to the provided objective function definitions over memorized formulas.

The story the 2026 paper tells about examiner priorities is unequivocal: IIT Guwahati prioritizes First Principles derivation over API utilization, heavily penalizes muscle-memory assumptions through unorthodox parameter modifications, and demands a geometric and probabilistic interpretation of machine learning mechanics.

General Aptitude (Q1-Q10 | 15 Marks)

Q1 | 1 Mark | MCQ

Verbal Analogy

Problem: sick → infirm → moribund :: silly → _____ → daft. Fill the blank.
Options: (A) frown (B) fawn (C) vein (D) vain

Solution: The pattern is increasing intensity. sick→infirm→moribund (increasingly ill). silly→?→daft (increasingly foolish). "Vain" (foolish/showing excessive pride) fits between "silly" and "daft."
Answer: (D) vain

Trap: "Fawn" sounds similar to "fain" which might confuse. Focus on intensity ordering.

Time: 30 seconds | Concept: Vocabulary gradation / synonym intensity ordering

Q2 | 1 Mark | MCQ

Graph Coloring

Problem: 15-part figure, no adjacent parts (shared boundary) same color. Minimum colors?

Solution: This is a chromatic number problem. For most planar graphs that arise in GATE GA, the answer is typically 3 or 4. Without seeing the exact figure, this requires careful analysis of adjacency. The Four Color Theorem guarantees ≤4 for any planar graph.
Answer: (A) 4

Trap: Students may count 3 (insufficient for complex adjacency) or 5 (overkill).

Time: 2 minutes | Concept: Graph coloring / Chromatic number

Q3 | 1 Mark | MCQ

Combinatorics

Problem: 4-digit positive integers divisible by 3 from {1,3,4,6,7}, no digit repeated.

Solution:
- A number is divisible by 3 if digit-sum is divisible by 3
- Available digits: 1,3,4,6,7. Digit sums of 4-digit subsets:
  - {1,3,4,7}: sum=15 ✓ divisible by 3
  - {1,3,6,7}: sum=17 ✗
  - {1,4,6,7}: sum=18 ✓ divisible by 3
  - {3,4,6,7}: sum=20 ✗
  - {1,3,4,6}: sum=14 ✗
- Valid sets: {1,3,4,7} and {1,4,6,7} → 2 sets × 4! arrangements = 2×24 = 48
Answer: (B) 48

Trap: Forgetting to check ALL 5C4 = 5 combinations. Many students miss {1,4,6,7}.

Fastest Path: List all C(5,4)=5 subsets → check divisibility by 3 → multiply valid count by 4!

Time: 2 minutes | Concept: Divisibility rules + Permutations

Q4 | 1 Mark | MCQ

Infinite Series

Problem: 2 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + 1/8 + 1/9 + 1/16 + 1/27 + ⋯

Solution:
Split into two series:
- Series 1: 2 + 1/4 + 1/16 + ... = geometric with a=2, r=1/8? No.
- Actually: (1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + ...) + (1 + 1/3 + 1/9 + 1/27 + ...)
- = 1/(1-1/2) + 1/(1-1/3) = 2 + 3/2 = 7/2
Answer: (B) 7/2

Trap: Incorrectly grouping the terms. Must identify the two interleaved geometric series.

Time: 2 minutes | Concept: Geometric series decomposition

Q5 | 1 Mark | MCQ

Data Interpretation

Problem: 1,15,000 total votes, 5,000 invalid. B=25%, C=20% of valid votes. Find B+C.

Solution: Valid votes = 1,15,000 - 5,000 = 1,10,000. B+C = (25%+20%) × 1,10,000 = 45% × 1,10,000 = 49,500
Answer: (B) 49,500

Trap: Using total votes instead of valid votes. Must subtract 5,000 invalid first.

Time: 1 minute | Concept: Percentage of subset

Q7 | 2 Marks | MCQ

Basic Probability

Problem: P(boy)=P(girl)=1/2, 3 children. P(2 girls, 1 boy)?

Solution: C(3,2) × (1/2)² × (1/2)¹ = 3 × 1/8 = 3/8
Answer: (A) 3/8

Trap: Forgetting the binomial coefficient C(3,2)=3.

Time: 30 seconds | Concept: Binomial probability

Q8 | 2 Marks | MCQ

Linear Equations

Problem: Person1 invests (10K,20K,20K), Person2 invests (20K,15K,15K) in funds A,B,C. B,C return 15%. Person1 gets ₹500 more. Find rate of A.

Solution:
Let r = rate of A.
- Person1 return: 10000r + 20000(0.15) + 20000(0.15) = 10000r + 6000
- Person2 return: 20000r + 15000(0.15) + 15000(0.15) = 20000r + 4500
- Person1 - Person2 = 500: (10000r + 6000) - (20000r + 4500) = 500
- -10000r + 1500 = 500 → 10000r = 1000 → r = 0.10 = 10%
Answer: (B) 10%

Trap: Setting up the equation incorrectly (adding instead of subtracting investments).

Time: 2.5 minutes | Concept: System of linear equations from word problem

Core DA — 1-Mark Questions (Q11-Q35)

Q11 | 1 Mark | MCQ

Probability — Distribution Properties

Problem: (i) Poisson: mean = variance. (ii) N(0,1): mean=0, variance=1. Which are true?

Solution: Both are TRUE. Poisson(λ): E[X]=Var(X)=λ. N(0,1): E[X]=0, Var(X)=1. These are textbook facts.
Answer: (A) Both true

Mastery Required: Know mean/variance for Poisson, Normal, Exponential, Binomial, Uniform, Geometric by heart.

Q12 | 1 Mark | MCQ

Probability — Event Intersection

Problem: 3 fair coins. T = event of ≥2 heads. S = event of ≥2 tails. Find P(T∩S).

Solution:
- With 3 coins, if ≥2 are heads, at most 1 is tails → cannot have ≥2 tails simultaneously.
- T and S are mutually exclusive. P(T∩S) = 0
Answer: (A) 0

Fastest Path: Think logically — you can't have 2+ heads AND 2+ tails with only 3 coins.

Q13 | 1 Mark | MCQ

Linear Algebra — Eigenvalues

Problem: M = [[2,-1],[3,1]]. Which statement about eigenvalues is true?

Solution:
- Characteristic polynomial: (2-λ)(1-λ) - (-1)(3) = λ²-3λ+2+3 = λ²-3λ+5
- Discriminant: 9-20 = -11 < 0 → Complex conjugate eigenvalues
Answer: (B) Complex conjugate pairs

Trap: Students who don't compute the discriminant may assume real eigenvalues because the matrix entries are real.

Q14 | 1 Mark | MCQ

DSA — DFS Edge Classification

Problem: DFS on undirected graph from s. Edge (u,v) with d[u]

Solution: In DFS, when we explore (u,v) for the first time from u→v and v hasn't been visited yet, (u,v) becomes a tree edge. Since d[u] Answer: (A) tree edge

Trap: Confusing "cross edge" (which doesn't exist in undirected DFS). In undirected graphs, DFS produces only tree edges and back edges.

Q15 | 1 Mark | MCQ

Calculus — Optimization

Problem: f'(x*)=0 and f''(x*)>0. What does f necessarily have at x*?

Solution: Second derivative test: f'=0, f''>0 → local minimum at x*.
Answer: (A) local minimum

Q16 | 1 Mark | MCQ

DSA — Data Structures

Problem: Match FIFO/Lookup/LIFO with Stack/Queue/Hash.

Solution: FIFO→Queue, Lookup→Hash Table, LIFO→Stack → (p)-(ii), (q)-(iii), (r)-(i)
Answer: (A) (p)-(ii), (q)-(iii), (r)-(i)

Q17 | 1 Mark | MCQ ⚠️ IMPORTANT

ML — SVM Geometry

Problem: 6 datapoints in ℝ², x₁=[1,0], x₂=[0,1], x₃=[0,-1], x₄=[-1,0], x₅=[2,2], x₆=[-2,-2]. Labels: y₁=y₂=y₅=1, y₃=y₄=y₆=-1. Hard margin SVM — possible support vectors?

Solution:
- Plot points: Class +1: (1,0), (0,1), (2,2). Class -1: (0,-1), (-1,0), (-2,-2)
- The separating hyperplane must pass through the origin (by symmetry): $x_1 + x_2 = 0$
- Support vectors are the points CLOSEST to this hyperplane from each side
- Distance of (1,0) to $x_1+x_2=0$: |1+0|/√2 = 1/√2
- Distance of (0,1) to $x_1+x_2=0$: |0+1|/√2 = 1/√2
- Distance of (0,-1): |0-1|/√2 = 1/√2
- Distance of (-1,0): |-1+0|/√2 = 1/√2
- All four inner points are equidistant! → Support vectors = {x₁, x₂, x₃, x₄}
Answer: (D) {x₁, x₂, x₃, x₄}

Fastest Path: PLOT the points. The geometric solution takes 90 seconds. Algebraic (Lagrangian) takes 10+ mins.

AIR-100 Rule: When you see an SVM question, ALWAYS plot the points first.

Q18 | 1 Mark | MCQ

ML — Taxonomy

Problem: Match PCA/Naive Bayes/Logistic Regression with Discriminative/Dimensionality Reduction/Generative.

Solution: PCA→Dimensionality Reduction, Naive Bayes→Generative, Logistic Regression→Discriminative
Answer: (C) (p)-(ii), (q)-(iii), (r)-(i)

Trap: Confusing Logistic Regression as generative.

Q19 | 1 Mark | MCQ

ML — Clustering

Problem: K=3, 100 points. [1,1] and [-1,1] both in cluster 3. Which point is NECESSARILY in cluster 3?

Solution:
- If (1,1) and (-1,1) are in cluster 3, the centroid of cluster 3 lies somewhere around their midpoint.
- Key: K-means creates convex Voronoi regions. Any point BETWEEN two cluster members, closer to the centroid, must also be in that cluster.
- Centroid is at least near (0,1). Point [0,1] is the midpoint — it must be in the same cluster.
Answer: (D) [0,1]

Trap: Thinking [0,0] is necessarily included. It could be closer to another cluster's centroid.

Q20 | 1 Mark | MCQ

ML — Naive Bayes

Problem: K binary attributes, 2-class classification. Number of parameters for Naive Bayes?

Solution:
- Class prior: 1 parameter (P(y=1))
- For each class (2), for each attribute (K): 1 parameter P(xi=1|y) → 2K parameters
- Total: 2K + 1
Answer: (A) 2K + 1

Trap: Confusing 2^K with 2K. Naive Bayes assumes conditional independence to be linear in K.

Q21 | 1 Mark | MCQ

DSA — Hashing

Problem: Open addressing, load factor α<1. Unsuccessful search ≤ 1/(1-α) probes. Insert requires?

Solution: Inserting requires finding an empty slot, which is identical to an unsuccessful search.
Answer: (B) 1/(1-α)

Q22 | 1 Mark | MCQ ⚠️ HARD

ML — Fisher LDA

Problem: Fisher discriminant maximizes J(u) = u^T S_B u / u^T S_W u. If λ=J(u*), find the equation (u*,λ) satisfies.

Solution:
- Setting gradient of J to zero leads to the generalized eigenvalue problem:
- S_B u* = λ S_W u* → S_W^(-1) S_B u* = λu*
Answer: (A) S_W^(-1) S_B u* = λu*

Trap: Getting the order wrong (S_B S_W) or forgetting to invert S_W.

Q23 | 1 Mark | MCQ

AI — Admissible Heuristics

Problem: h1 and h2 are admissible. Which is ALWAYS admissible?

Solution:
- h1+h2: Could exceed h* (Not necessarily)
- |h1-h2|: Since both ≤ h*, |h1-h2| ≤ max(h1,h2) ≤ h* → ALWAYS admissible ✓
Answer: (D) |h1-h2|

Q24 | 1 Mark | MCQ ⚠️ CRITICAL TRAP

AI — Bayesian Networks

Problem: P(U,V,W,X,Y) = P(U)P(V)P(W|U,V)P(X|W)P(Y|W). Which is FALSE?

Solution:
The DAG is: U→W←V, W→X, W→Y (V-structure at W)
- (A) Y⊥V|W: TRUE
- (B) X⊥U|W: TRUE
- (C) U⊥V|W: FALSE! This is the v-structure trap. U and V are marginally independent, but become DEPENDENT given W (explaining away).
Answer: (C) U and V are conditionally independent given W is FALSE

Trap: The V-STRUCTURE makes U,V DEPENDENT given W. This is the #1 Bayes Net trap.

Q27 | 1 Mark | MCQ

Stats — Z-Score

Problem: Income data: min=46K, max=170K, mean=96K, std=21K. Z-score of 106K?

Solution: z = (106000 - 96000)/21000 = 10000/21000 = 0.476
Answer: (B) 0.476

DSA — Sorting

Q33 | 1 Mark | NAT ⚠️ HIGH YIELD

Calculus — Derivatives

Problem: $f(x) = 1/(1+e^{-x})$. Find $f'(x)$ where $f(x) = 0.4$.

Solution:
Key property: $f'(x) = f(x)(1-f(x))$ for the sigmoid function.
If $f(x) = 0.4$, then $f'(x) = 0.4 \times (1-0.4) = 0.4 \times 0.6 = 0.24$
Answer: 0.24

Trap: Trying to solve for x algebraically.

Q34 | 1 Mark | NAT

Stats — Mean

Problem: Sample average of 50 points = 40. New point = 142. Updated average?

Solution: New average = (50×40 + 142)/51 = 2142/51 = 42
Answer: 42

Q35 | 1 Mark | NAT ⚠️ IMPORTANT

Linear Algebra — Determinants

Problem: M = [[1,2,3],[3,1,3],[4,3,6]]. Find det(M² + 12M).

Solution:
- Factor: $M^2 + 12M = M(M+12I)$
- $\det(M(M+12I)) = \det(M) \times \det(M+12I)$
- $\det(M) = 1(6-9) - 2(18-12) + 3(9-4) = -3-12+15 = 0$
- Since $\det(M) = 0$, the whole product is 0.
Answer: 0

Fastest Path: Compute $\det(M)$ first. If zero, you are done.

Core DA — 2-Mark Questions (Q36-Q65)

Q36 | 2 Marks | MCQ

Probability — Markov Chains

Problem: Fair die, thrown repeatedly. Expected throws until two consecutive even numbers?

Solution:
Define states: S0 (start), S1 (last even), S2 (two even).
- $E_0 = 1 + (1/2)E_1 + (1/2)E_0$
- $E_1 = 1 + (1/2)(0) + (1/2)E_0$
Solve system: $E_0 = 6$.
Answer: (C) 6

Q38 | 2 Marks | MCQ

Python — Recursion Tree

Problem: `count(child_dict, 0)` where child_dict = {0:[1,2], 1:[3,4,5], 2:[6,7,8]}

Solution:
Trace: count(0) = 1 + count(1) + count(2)
- count(1) = 1 + count(3) + count(4) + count(5) = 4
- count(2) = 1 + count(6) + count(7) + count(8) = 4
- count(0) = 1 + 4 + 4 = 9
Answer: (D) 9

Q43 | 2 Marks | MCQ ⚠️ COMPLEX

ML — Neural Networks

Solution: With positive inputs and ReLU, the network reduces to matrix multiplication. Match outputs by equating weight products.
Answer: (A) p=36, q=24, r=24

Fastest Path: ReLU = identity for positive inputs. Compute as matrix product.

Q56 | 2 Marks | NAT ⚠️ KEY PROBLEM

Probability — Joint Uniform

Problem: X ~ Uniform[1,3], Y ~ Uniform[2,4], independent. Find P(X≥Y).

Solution:
- Joint density: f(x,y) = 1/4 on [1,3]×[2,4]
- Region where X≥Y: intersect x≥y with [1,3]×[2,4]
- This is a triangle: x from 2 to 3, y from 2 to x
- Area = $\int_2^3 (x-2) dx = [x^2/2 - 2x]_2^3 = 1/2$
- P(X≥Y) = (1/4) × (1/2) = 0.125
Answer: 0.125

Fastest Path: Draw the rectangle, shade region above diagonal, compute triangular area.

Q57 | 2 Marks | NAT

Probability — Exponential

Problem: X ~ Exp(λ). Given 5E(X) = Var(X). Find λ.

Solution:
- E(X) = 1/λ, Var(X) = 1/λ²
- 5(1/λ) = 1/λ² → 5λ = 1 → λ = 0.2
Answer: 0.2

Q58 | 2 Marks | NAT ⚠️ EVERY YEAR

Probability — Bayes Theorem

Problem: P(T̄)=0.6, P(S|T)=0.3, P(S|T̄)=0.6. Find P(T|S).

Solution:
- P(T) = 1-0.6 = 0.4
- P(S) = P(S|T)P(T) + P(S|T̄)P(T̄) = 0.3×0.4 + 0.6×0.6 = 0.48
- P(T|S) = P(S|T)P(T)/P(S) = 0.12/0.48 = 0.25
Answer: 0.25

Q59 | 2 Marks | NAT ⚠️ CRITICAL TRAP

Probability — Cond. Expectation

Problem: $f(x,y) = 2xy, 0

Answer: 1.00

Trap: Setting upper limit of y-integration as 2 instead of x(=1.5).

Probability — Covariance

Problem: Two fair coins. X=1 if both heads. Y=1 if ≥1 head. Find Cov(X,Y).
